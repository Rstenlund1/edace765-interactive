Executive Summary

AI can help a gamified learning system adjust practice, feedback, and content to individual learners. That makes it worth considering for adult education, where learners bring different experience, responsibilities, and goals. It does not establish that an AI system teaches better than a well-designed alternative.

Our recommendation is conditional adoption: begin with a clear learning need, test the added value of AI, and keep learners involved in consequential decisions. Using andragogy and self-directed learning as our two foundations, we examine four connected issues: personalization and learning; agency and dependence; privacy and transparency; and equity and access. Research supports the potential of gamification, but evidence about gamification alone cannot establish the additional benefit of AI. We use three examples in different ways: Duolingo's Birdbrain as a documented platform feature, the Army's after-action review as a design pattern we adapt for AI, and one learner's reported experience using Claude to study anatomy.

The practical test is whether learners gain useful capability while retaining meaningful influence over their learning. A system that produces more clicks has answered a different question.

Milestone: The machine we built

Who Made Who?

The drums come first - a dry, gated snare and a floor tom locked into a militant, mid-tempo march. Steady, mechanical, and unmistakably AC/DC.

The rhythm guitar enters, and Brian Johnson starts singing about a video game. The player faces the machine one-on-one. Another dime disappears. The game wins again. What begins as a person controlling a machine becomes something more unsettling. The player may be playing the game, but the game has gotten its hooks into the player, too.

Then Angus Young's guitar comes in as fast and hard as one of the driverless semis gone mad in Stephen King's Maximum Overdrive. The song appeared on the film's 1986 soundtrack album (AC/DC, 1986). As the music builds, the question at the center of the song finally lands:

Who made who?

Ain't nobody told you?

It is a surprisingly good question for modern gamification.

Human beings create systems to influence human behavior. We establish goals, measure progress, assign scores, create ranks, and recognize accomplishments. Then we change our behavior because those systems exist. People made the system, and then the system changed what people did.

Modern gamification lives somewhere in that relationship. AI makes it more complicated because a system can use patterns in our responses to change what it offers us next.

Making Progress Visible

Human beings have the peculiar habit of taking invisible effort and turning it into something we can count, rank, display, and chase. Think of apprentices becoming journeymen and masters, soldiers moving through ranks, or students working toward degrees. Those structures are not automatically gamification. They illustrate the much broader habit of making advancement visible.

Visible progress can matter. Harkin et al. (2016), in a meta-analysis of 138 studies involving 19,951 participants, found that interventions designed to increase progress monitoring also increased goal attainment. This was evidence about monitoring goals, not a test of gamification itself. Seeing where we are does not guarantee that we will reach where we are going, but it can affect what we do next.

Gamification Has a Branding Problem

The word itself makes the idea sound sillier than it is. Say gamification to an adult learner, and they may picture cartoon badges, classroom competitions, or an instructor desperately trying to make professional development “fun.” The problem is built directly into the name.

Put simply, gamification borrows pieces of games without turning the activity itself into a game. Landers (2014) describes using game attributes outside games to influence learning-related attitudes and behaviors. A workplace module with a completion badge is still workplace training.

▦ Three structures that can look alike

Visible progress: A record shows how far someone has advanced. A degree or a rank is not automatically gamification.

Gamification: Game elements support another activity. A practice module might use challenges, points, or badges.

Game-based learning: Learning occurs through playing a game. A simulation belongs here when it is designed as a game; simulations are not all games.

One learner may find a game engaging. Another may find the same activity childish, forced, or disconnected from why they showed up. Gamification does not require disguising adult education as recess. At its simplest, it may help the learner answer: Where am I? Where am I going? How will I know when I get there?

The Gamification We Stop Seeing

Perhaps the most familiar achievement systems are the ones we stop noticing. Fitness trackers close activity rings. Language apps maintain streaks. Ordinary effort becomes something measurable, and there is always another finish line.

Credit scores offer a stranger analogy. Almost nobody wakes up thinking, “I cannot wait to play Credit Score today,” yet the visible number and thresholds can become targets in themselves. Credit scoring is a consequential financial assessment, not an example of educational gamification. The resemblance is in what happens when a number starts organizing our behavior.

The machinery has been running around us for so long that most of us no longer hear it.

When the Marker Becomes the Motivation

A finish line can pull someone forward, but it can also make them forget why they started running. Adults enter education for wildly different reasons: a promotion, a credential, a new career, or the need to prove they can finish something difficult. Several motives can exist at once.

An external reward may sustain effort when every step is not intrinsically interesting. A certification can show that someone met a professional standard. A degree can turn years of work into something visible. The marker does not contain the effort; it represents it.

The breakdown happens when the marker stops representing the goal and becomes the target. Students learn how to protect a grade, calculate the exact effort required to pass, maintain a streak, or stack credentials. If gamification drives participation and completion, it has accomplished something.

Worked at what?

◉ What did we actually measure?

Participation: Did the learner return or finish?

Performance: Could the learner complete the task under the conditions provided?

Learning and transfer: Does the capability last, and can the learner use it in a different situation?

These are related outcomes. One is not proof of the next.

A learner can become exceptionally skilled at navigating the reward structure without mastering what it was meant to teach.

Research gives us reason for optimism without permitting complacency. Triantafyllou et al. (2025) emphasize that gamification's effectiveness depends on instructional design and context. Xu et al. (2025), in a meta-analysis of 143 effect sizes from 32 higher-education studies, found a moderate positive effect on learning outcomes overall. Results varied with the design, domain, duration, and outcomes measured. Neither review establishes that adding AI will improve those results.

For the adult educator, “Did they engage?” cannot be the final question. Did they understand? Can they apply what they learned when the rewards are gone?

There Is No Such Thing as “The Adult Learner”

Of course adult learners exist, but there is no single interchangeable person who walks into every classroom carrying the same experiences, motivations, abilities, and expectations. One may already know half the material. Another may be encountering it for the first time. One wants detailed guidance; another desperately wants the instructor to get out of the way.

A leaderboard can awaken the competitive streak in one learner while making another wonder why someone turned professional development into The Hunger Games. They have not necessarily approached education incorrectly. They have brought different histories and expectations into the same room.

Educators can account for those differences through optional challenges, varied feedback, multiple pathways, and adjustments to difficulty. Adaptation did not begin with AI. Teachers and systems using predefined rules can already change what happens next. The practical limitation of a fixed design is the amount of ongoing adjustment it can provide.

A Score Can Tell You That Someone Failed, But It Cannot Tell You Why

A learner may miss a question because the material is difficult, the wording is confusing, or their attention wandered. The score alone does not settle which explanation is right.

A good educator can sometimes recognize the difference. When one explanation fails, another can take its place. A movie, song, workplace problem, ridiculous analogy, or well-timed joke can bridge something unfamiliar and something the learner already understands.

Personalization is part of good teaching, but personalization and teaching are not synonyms. Changing the difficulty of a quiz is personalization. Knowing why the learner needs something different requires more than a score.

Marengo et al. (2025) identify personalized educational pathways as one use of AI alongside gamification. AI can help a system find patterns and propose adjustments at a scale that would be difficult for an instructor to manage alone. Those patterns are still interpretations of limited data. The system has not acquired a complete understanding of the person.

This introduces a problem. A system designed to support learning and one designed to maximize compliance may use similar tools: timing, difficulty, feedback, rewards, and pathways. The crucial difference is whose goal those tools serve. An employer can legitimately set a required standard; that does not settle how much control the learner should retain in reaching it.

Same tools. Different master.

Humans built systems to influence behavior, and those systems changed what humans did. Now we are building systems that study those responses and adjust themselves accordingly. The machine in the song waited for another dime. An intelligent system can begin estimating why we put the dime in.

So what happens when the system begins learning the learner?

Milestone: The system begins learning the learner

What Changes When AI Enters the Picture?

Integrating AI can make gamification more responsive by adapting content, difficulty, or feedback to patterns in learner activity. Generative AI adds the ability to produce new material (Abbes et al., 2024), while predictive models can estimate future performance from response patterns (Piech et al., 2015). These functions can work together, but they are not interchangeable.

The distinction is not simply static versus intelligent. Non-AI systems can provide immediate feedback and branching pathways. AI adds other ways to estimate learner needs or generate responses; educational value still depends on the design. Reviews describe promising applications, while an opinion article by Patiño Barriga et al. (2026) argues for integrating them through deliberate pedagogical choices. Adult educators need evidence about the particular design they intend to use.

What AI may add
Dimension Fixed or rule-based design What AI may add
Adaptation An instructor specifies pathways or branching rules. A model estimates which task or level of difficulty may be useful next.
Content Examples and scenarios are prepared in advance. Generative models can draft additional examples, questions, or dialogue.
Feedback Feedback can already be immediate and detailed. A model can respond to open-ended work or generate follow-up prompts.
Game elements Points, badges, stories, and collaboration can support different purposes. Their timing or presentation can change using learner data.
Motivation Outcomes depend on relevance, choices, and implementation. Personalization may help; intrinsic motivation and sustained learning are not automatic results.

Does AI Solve Gamification's Problems, and How?

Personalization & Adaptive Learning

Learner profiling uses available information, such as prior answers and activity history, to estimate what support might be useful. AI may help tailor pace, examples, practice, or feedback. It cannot reliably interpret every hesitation as boredom, confusion, or lack of knowledge.

All three roles may change:

  • The system can recommend support. It can recommend practice or offer a cue when a pattern suggests difficulty. The recommendation remains an inference that may need correction.
  • The learner can help shape the pathway. Learners can request examples, explain their goals, and challenge a recommendation. Responding to a learner's behavior does not itself grant that learner control.
  • The instructor's role changes. Automation may reduce some repetitive work, but checking generated content, resolving errors, and monitoring outcomes create responsibilities of their own.

Reviews of AI and gamification describe this potential for personalization (Abbes et al., 2024; Marengo et al., 2025). The educator still decides what the learning experience should accomplish.

Feedback, Remediation & Changing Pathways

AI can supplement formative assessment by identifying patterns, generating practice, and suggesting feedback. It does not replace the need for valid assessments or professional judgment.

Deep knowledge tracing uses sequences of learner responses to predict future performance (Piech et al., 2015). A prediction can help select a task, but it does not directly reveal why an answer was wrong. Similarly, a generative model may propose that an error reflects a misconception, but an instructor or learner must be able to check that interpretation.

Concept card: Deep knowledge tracing
What it can do: Use a sequence of learner responses to predict future performance.

What it cannot establish by itself: Why the learner made an error or which underlying misconception caused it.

Feedback can take the form of a question, a hint, or a worked example. Which is appropriate depends on the learning goal and the learner's current needs. Awarding points for effort does not, by itself, establish that the system has improved motivation or understanding.

⇄ A recommendation the learner can inspect

Observe: Several recent answers were incorrect.

Estimate: The model predicts that more practice may help.

Propose: Review this concept or try another example.

Check together: Does that recommendation fit the learner's explanation and goal?

This is a proposed design pattern, not a description of every AI platform.

Holding the Learner in the Challenge Zone

Adaptive practice can offer additional support when work is too difficult and greater challenge when it is too easy. Error patterns and response times may help inform those adjustments, but they are imperfect measures of a learner's experience.

Keeping a learner successful on every attempt is not the same as helping them learn. A useful system must leave room for retrieval, uncertainty, and problems that require effort. The learner and instructor should be able to question a recommendation to lower the difficulty.

What AI Cannot Resolve on Its Own

AI cannot make an irrelevant curriculum meaningful merely by personalizing it. Success inside a platform does not establish transfer to a workplace, and generated feedback requires checking. Burgos-Pereira et al. (2025) review potential applications in organizational training, but a review of promising approaches is not proof that a particular product works for a particular workforce.

Implementation also depends on understandable recommendations, suitable data, and evaluation beyond engagement. Zhaxybayev et al. (2026) identify gaps in explainability, integrated assessment of critical thinking, and resources for underrepresented languages. Those findings should guide questions about a proposed system, rather than become precise claims about every educational AI product.

Example 1: Duolingo's Birdbrain

Documented platform feature; not independent proof of learning effectiveness.

Duolingo's technical account describes Birdbrain as a model that predicts the difficulty of an exercise for a particular learner. Its lesson generator can use those predictions when selecting exercises (Bicknell & Brust, 2020). The app's game elements, such as streaks, are a separate part of the experience. AI-generated content, adaptive exercise selection, and gamification should not be treated as the same feature.

Duolingo also documented separating daily goals from the conditions for maintaining a streak (Yu, 2020). That is a useful reminder that changing a reward rule can change participation without establishing what learners retained. These dated accounts illustrate design choices; features may differ across versions of the app.

For an adult educator, the question is whether the recommended practice addresses a learning need and whether the learner can understand or influence the process. That brings the learner back into the loop.

Adult Learner in the Loop

🎯 The Adult Learner in the Loop

Honey

Section path: 3 of 475% through the four main sections

Milestone: Put the adult learner back in the loop

The design of automated systems uses specific vocabulary to describe where humans sit in relation to a machine. Research on levels of automation asks how much of a decision a system makes and how much stays with its operator (Parasuraman et al., 2000). The terms in the loop, on the loop, and out of the loop also appear in discussions of autonomous military systems (Human Rights Watch & International Human Rights Clinic, 2012).

Applied here, the difference is who holds decision authority. A learner can supply a system with data while having little influence over the decisions made from those data.

Our two adult-learning foundations are andragogy and self-directed learning. Together, they help us ask whether personalization respects adults' purposes and develops their ability to direct learning. The supporting studies below examine particular design choices rather than add further foundational theories.

Andragogy

How do core principles of adult learning fit into intelligent gamification?

Knowles's andragogical model describes adults' need to know why they are learning, their self-concept as responsible decision-makers, their experience, readiness to learn, problem orientation, and motivation (Knowles et al., 2015). These are working assumptions, not descriptions that fit every adult in every situation.

Andragogy and adaptive gamification
Andragogical assumption What AI-adaptive gamification can offer Where it can work against the principle
Need to know Explain why a task or review was assigned. Give no reason for changing the pathway.
Self-concept Offer meaningful choices of pace and challenge. Decide silently and call the result personalized.
Experience Build examples around the learner's field and invite reflection. Treat experience only as data to mine.
Readiness Connect practice to a current work or life need. Push content because participation has dipped.
Problem orientation Organize challenges around useful problems. Make maintaining the reward the main problem to solve.
Motivation Support a sense of competence and a personally meaningful purpose. Tune incentives only to keep the learner clicking.

Intelligent gamification can meet the letter of andragogy while missing its spirit. Individualization is only part of the issue. Knowles's process model involves learners in diagnosing needs, setting objectives, and evaluating results. Profiling can support that process when learners can inspect and question it. When the diagnosis happens invisibly, the experience may be personalized to the learner without being developed with the learner.

Self-Directed Learning

Garrison's (1997) model brings together three dimensions: self-management, or control over the learning task and its context; self-monitoring, or responsibility for constructing meaning and checking understanding; and motivation, including both beginning and persisting with learning.

Adaptive systems can support each dimension, but predicting a learner's performance is not the same as that learner reflecting on understanding. Recommending a pathway is not the same as the learner deciding how to proceed. If the system makes every plan and interprets every result, the learner loses opportunities to practice those decisions.

Darvishi et al. (2024) examined this concern in a randomized experiment involving 1,625 university students across 10 courses. The system prompted students to improve peer feedback. After an initial period with AI assistance, groups received continued prompts, no prompts, self-monitoring checklists, or both supports. Students tended to rely on the assistance rather than carry the same behavior forward without it. Checklists helped fill part of the gap but were less effective than continued AI assistance; combining both did not outperform AI alone. This was a study of peer feedback, not proof that every AI tutor creates dependence.

Practice can also feel successful without producing durable learning. Bjork and Bjork (2011) describe desirable difficulties: conditions such as spacing practice, varying practice, and retrieving information that can make practice harder while supporting retention. The difficulty must be manageable. Confusion and inaccessible instruction are not desirable merely because they are difficult.

Concept card: Desirable difficulty
The idea: Some effortful conditions, such as retrieval, spacing, or varied practice, can support durable learning even when practice feels harder.

The boundary: Confusion, inaccessible instruction, or unnecessary barriers are not desirable simply because they are difficult.

Applied to adaptive gamification, this means the educator should ask what the system is optimizing. Constantly removing uncertainty may improve immediate performance while reducing opportunities to judge an answer or retrieve it independently. That is a reason to evaluate the timing and purpose of support, not a rule that immediate feedback is harmful.

Grow's (1991) model offers one way to adjust support within self-directed learning. It describes dependent, interested, involved, and self-directed learners, with teaching roles ranging from direct guidance to consultation. Those positions should not become permanent labels. A person may need substantial guidance in an unfamiliar subject while directing learning confidently elsewhere.

An adaptive system can therefore adjust how much responsibility it asks the learner to take, as well as task difficulty. The aim is to build the judgment needed for the learning goal. It need not remove an accessibility tool or useful professional aid to prove that learning occurred.

Autonomy & Learner Control

Sailer et al. (2017) tested combinations of game elements in a randomized study. Badges, leaderboards, and performance graphs supported participants' sense of competence and task meaningfulness. Avatars, a meaningful story, and teammates supported social relatedness. The researchers did not produce the intended effect on perceived decision freedom.

That result matters here. Feeling engaged in meaningful work and having authority over a learning decision are different things. Giving someone three pathways does not tell us who selected those pathways, whether another is possible, or whether declining carries a penalty.

◎ Where is the learner in this decision?

In the loop: The system proposes a change; the learner accepts, rejects, or helps revise it.

On the loop: The system adjusts automatically; the learner can see what changed and override it.

Out of the loop: A decision is made without the learner's involvement or a meaningful way to challenge it.

A single platform can use all three arrangements for different decisions.

Not every adjustment needs approval. Nobody wants to sign off on every spaced-repetition interval. The problem arises when a learner believes they control a consequential decision but cannot see or change it.

Motivation, Relevance & Experience

A generative system can draft a case around a learner's industry, role, or recent challenge. A good educator bridges the unfamiliar and familiar with a story, memory, or analogy, hanging information on hooks the learner already has. AI can attempt that bridge at scale. Someone still needs to check that the example is accurate and actually relevant.

Adult learning also happens in mandated settings such as safety training, compliance, and required professional development. Here, the learner does not choose every goal. The organization can legitimately require a standard while allowing choices about practice, pacing within a deadline, or how the learner demonstrates understanding.

Sailer and Homner's (2020) meta-analysis found positive average effects of gamification on cognitive, motivational, and behavioral outcomes, with differences across designs and studies. Some findings favored combining competition with collaboration. That supports testing purposeful designs; it does not establish the best design for every required training program.

Slade and Prinsloo (2013) argue for treating learners as agents in the collection and use of learning data. Learners need to understand what is being collected, why, and who can access it. Profiles should also remain open to correction. A prediction made early in a course should not harden into a permanent label.

The question is whether the learner is a partner in that observation or only its object.

Example 2: The US Army's After-Action Review as a Design Pattern

Established training practice; the AI-supported version below is a proposal.

The Army's after-action review uses a guided discussion of a training event to identify what happened, why it happened, and how to improve (Department of the Army, 1993). Its familiar questions can be adapted for an adult-learning activity:

  • What was supposed to happen?
  • What actually happened?
  • Why was there a difference?
  • What should we sustain or improve?

An AI-supported version could help assemble a timeline or summarize recorded decisions. Participants would check that record, explain their reasoning, and decide what to change. If the training uses game elements, the discussion could also examine whether a reward encouraged the intended behavior.

The after-action review is not itself evidence that AI-enhanced gamification works. It gives us a design pattern that keeps interpretation with the learner while assigning the machine a supporting role.

Example 3: A Learner Who Built His Own Loop

One learner's reported experience.

Jordan is a 32-year-old personal trainer working toward a career as a physical therapist. He uses a general-purpose AI assistant, Claude, to build a quiz game for his anatomy course (J. Rice, personal communication, September 19, 2026).

↻ Jordan's study cycle

1. Build a set. Upload course worksheets and request ten questions organized by category.

2. Attempt the questions. Answer from memory and review the assistant's feedback.

3. Judge understanding. Examine missed answers and answers that were correct but uncertain.

4. Choose the next focus. Supply more course material and request practice on the areas he identifies.

The reported method leaves the next learning decision with Jordan. Checking AI feedback against course materials remains necessary.

His questions became more complex over time: muscle names, then actions, origins and insertions, and relationships among muscles. Jordan chose that progression. It suggests a move beyond isolated recall, although the question format and the quality of his explanations would be needed to judge the depth of learning.

Jordan is explicit about wanting to recall the material rather than look it up later. Research on retrieval practice supports that distinction: Roediger and Karpicke (2006) found that retrieving studied material could produce better delayed retention than repeated study under their experimental conditions.

Jordan reports scoring 100% on his week-three anatomy exam and credits the drilling. He also reports finding the method more engaging than flashcards. This is encouraging, but it is his account of one early assessment. It does not establish that AI caused the result or that the knowledge will transfer to clinical reasoning.

Several features connect his method to the adult-learning foundations:

  • Self-monitoring stays with the learner. Jordan decides what a missed or uncertain answer means and chooses the next focus. He is practicing the judgment described in Garrison's model.
  • A score is not the only signal. A correct answer can be a guess. Jordan's uncertainty check adds information, but confidence itself can be inaccurate. Research on overconfidence shows why learners should compare their judgments with dependable evidence (Dunlosky & Rawson, 2012).
  • The purpose is concrete. His anatomy study connects with a career goal and his experience as a trainer. That gives the learning an immediate anchor.
  • The learner helps design the activity. Categories and scores can be incorporated into a gameful study routine. A quiz with ten questions is not automatically gamification; the relevant point is how he uses game elements to structure practice.

The AI's feedback is not an objective answer key. Uploading course materials gives it a reference, but instructions to use only those materials do not guarantee that it will follow them accurately. The instructor's materials and approved answers remain the standard for checking a disputed response.

A less experienced learner may need help designing useful questions, checking feedback, and interpreting uncertainty. Jordan's account illustrates learner control; it does not establish a formula that will work equally well for everyone.

⚖️ The Benefits, Risks & Real-World Test

Lauren

Section path: 4 of 4100% through the four main sections

Milestone: Decide whether the added value justifies the added risk

AI can make gamification more responsive and potentially more useful to adult learners. It can also make the same systems more observant, more persuasive, and more capable of making decisions about people without their full awareness.

The harder question is: Better for what, better for whom, and at what ultimate cost?

1. Personalization & Meaningful Learning

Research on AI and gamification describes potential benefits from more responsive feedback and learning pathways (Marengo et al., 2025). Broader gamification research reports positive average effects on learning, although effectiveness varies with design and context (Triantafyllou et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025).

For adults, useful adaptation might provide extra practice, connect examples to professional goals, or accommodate different starting points. But personalization should serve learning. A system is not educationally better simply because it changes more frequently or knows more about the learner.

It can become very good at increasing clicks, completion rates, streaks, or quiz scores without improving the outcome the educator actually cares about. As Richard asks, “Worked at what?”

Practical response: Define the skill before selecting the platform. Compare the AI-supported design with a reasonable alternative, such as the same practice with prepared feedback. Check retention and application after practice, using the tools appropriate to the real task. The added AI should earn its place.

2. Agency, Manipulation & Dependence

Gamification influences behavior. AI can make that influence more individualized. Susser et al. (2019) discuss manipulation as covert influence that undermines a person's ability to make decisions, including by exploiting vulnerabilities.

That does not mean every reminder or recommendation is manipulation. Educators have always structured learning environments. Our concern grows when the learner cannot see how steering occurs, cannot meaningfully question it, and is being directed toward a purpose they do not understand. These are warning signs for review, not a validated diagnostic formula.

A highly adaptive system can also become a very effective scaffold. Darvishi et al. (2024) give us reason to ask whether learners internalize the processes assistance supports. An assistant might invite learners to identify a weak area before revealing its analysis, or gradually hand more planning decisions back to them.

Practical response: Explain the goal and give learners meaningful control over consequential adjustments. Ask them to interpret results and plan next steps. Reduce instructional scaffolding when doing so supports the goal; preserve assistive technology and other tools learners appropriately use in practice. Independence should not become an excuse to remove access.

3. Privacy & Transparency

Personalization requires information. That may include answers, mistakes, response times, preferences, or patterns of participation. It does not follow that a system needs every available piece of information.

Regan and Jesse (2019) examine concerns about privacy, surveillance, autonomy, and sorting learners through personalized educational technologies. In workplace learning, a prediction used to recommend practice may have different consequences if it is also sent to a supervisor.

Questions should be visible rather than buried in a privacy policy: What is collected, why is it needed, who can see it, and how long is it retained? Can learners inspect or correct an inference? Can optional personalization be declined without losing access to the training?

Practical response: Limit collection to the learning purpose, define access and retention, and separate practice feedback from consequential employment judgments. Provide a person learners can contact when a profile or recommendation is wrong.

4. Equity & Accessibility

Personalization can sound inherently equitable because each learner receives something different. But different does not automatically mean fair.

Baker and Hawn (2022) review evidence that bias can enter educational AI at multiple points. They describe findings involving race and ethnicity, gender, nationality, socioeconomic status, disability, and other characteristics, while noting gaps in the evidence.

Imagine two adults who struggle on the same assessment. One receives another chance at an advanced task; the other is repeatedly routed into remedial work. This is a hypothetical example, but it shows why an incorrect prediction could restrict opportunity rather than provide useful support.

Practical response: Examine outcomes across relevant learner groups, check keyboard and screen-reader access, and provide workable alternatives for learners with limited connectivity or language support. Learners need a way to challenge an inappropriate classification. A prediction should inform a decision, not become the learner's identity.

Concept card: Prediction is not identity
A prediction: A provisional estimate based on available data that may help inform support or a decision.

The risk: Treating that estimate as a fixed description of the learner can turn an uncertain model output into a durable label.

The Real-World Test

AI-enhanced gamification does not need to pass a test of perfection. It does need to demonstrate enough educational value to justify the additional information, influence, and work it requires.

The real-world test
Test Question to ask Warning sign
Learning & Capability Does the learning last, transfer to the intended task, and help learners perform with the tools appropriate to that task? Success is defined mainly by clicks, streaks, or completion, or performance depends on guidance that will not be available when needed.
Agency Does the learner understand the goal and have meaningful influence? The system silently decides what the learner needs.
Transparency Can learners see what is collected, inferred, and shared? Profiles and consequential predictions are invisible.
Equity Does the design work for different learners, with accessible alternatives? Untested predictions restrict opportunity.

Jordan's account illustrates the distinction. It offers a promising account of agency because he selects the next topic. His reported exam score is limited evidence of learning. The account does not tell us enough about data settings, accessibility, or longer-term transfer to clear those parts of the test. One strong feature cannot answer every question.

● Green Light, Yellow Light, Red Light

GREEN - A justified, monitored use. The learning purpose is clear, evidence supports the intended use, learners have meaningful control, and data and access arrangements are understood.

YELLOW - Investigate or redesign. The system appears helpful, but evidence is mainly engagement data, explanations are unclear, or learners have limited ways to challenge decisions.

RED - Do not adopt as designed. Hidden steering, unjustified data sharing, inaccessible participation, or unchallengeable consequential predictions outweigh the claimed benefit.

A green light applies to a defined use, not to every feature of a product.

So, Is the System Adapting to the Learner?

AI can overcome some practical limits of fixed gamification by adjusting practice, feedback, and content. It can enhance useful features when those adjustments address an actual learning need. It introduces new risks when greater responsiveness depends on opaque profiling, unequal treatment, or decisions learners cannot question.

Our recommendation is to adopt it conditionally: establish the purpose, evaluate the added value, and keep people able to inspect and change consequential decisions. Adults also need the digital literacy to question generated answers, instructors need time to review the system, and institutions need to account for cost and access. These conditions affect whether a promising design is feasible in practice.

Learners will adapt as they learn. That alone is not the problem. The question is whether they understand and can influence the purpose of that adaptation, and whether the capability they gain is worth what the system asks of them.
